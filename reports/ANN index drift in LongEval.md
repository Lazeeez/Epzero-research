# Novel gap, overstated premises: fix before pitching

The proposal is not yet bulletproof, but its core is sound and the gap is real. **No LongEval paper from 2023 to 2026 has run an approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) first stage, reported index parameters, compared approximate with exact search, or attributed effectiveness change to the index.** No published work decomposes longitudinal IR effectiveness into an encoder/data term and an index term. The streaming-ANN literature measures index decay only as recall against exact k-NN on synthetic or benchmark runbooks, never as nDCG on time-ordered relevance-judged snapshots. The intersection is unoccupied. Five things in the draft would draw fire from an informed supervisor, though, and four of them are contradicted by sources. LongEval does not "treat the approximation as exact": its official dense baseline *is* exact, a brute-force NumPy search rebuilt for every snapshot, so no published LongEval result contains index error that could be misattributed. "Little evidence on when rebuilding is needed" ignores a large systems literature (FreshDiskANN, SPFresh, IP-DiskANN, Quake, DeDrift, and a 2026 paper finding that signal-triggered repair does not beat a fixed cadence). The compressed-vector story is backwards for product quantization, where codebooks barely degrade under drift and retraining helps nothing measurable. The query-drift ratio is a known statistic, not a new one. "A well-configured index loses very little" is wrong at multi-million scale. The fix is a reframing, not a new project. Pitch it as the first measurement, on real evolving IR collections with judgements, of what the deployed approximate index adds to longitudinal drift, and of which maintenance actions recover which part of it. Treat the decomposition as an accounting identity with an interaction term, not a causal split. Narrow the novelty claims to scalar-quantization range staleness, HNSW-specific maintenance, and a size-controlled drift signal.

## The intersection is empty, and the LongEval record proves it

The strongest evidence for novelty comes from a full-text search of every LongEval working-notes paper in 2025 and 2026, not from abstracts. **None of the 12 LongEval 2026 papers in CEUR-WS Vol-4283 uses ANN search for dense retrieval, and in Task 1 all 20 submitted runs from eight teams use a lexical ranker at some point, with only 3 using a dense neural component** ([Extended Overview of CLEF 2026 LongEval](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)). The only appearance of "approximate nearest neighbor" in the 2026 overview describes how the Task 4 RAG questions were constructed with SPECTER2 embeddings, which is dataset construction, not retrieval ([paper374](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)). The organisers' Qwen3-Embedding-4B baseline builds a pyterrier-dr `FlexIndex` for each snapshot and retrieves with `index.retriever()` ([baseline.py](https://github.com/clef-longeval/longeval-code/blob/main/clef26/scientific-retrieval/baseline-pyterrier-dense/baseline.py)). Its run metadata records `NumpyRetriever` and "leave everything to the defaults" ([ir-metadata.yml](https://github.com/clef-longeval/longeval-code/blob/main/clef26/scientific-retrieval/baseline-pyterrier-dense/baseline-qwen3-embedding-4b/ir-metadata.yml)), and pyterrier-dr documents that retriever as "an exhaustive (exact) search using numpy" ([pyterrier-dr README](https://github.com/terrierteam/pyterrier_dr); [np_retr.py](https://github.com/terrierteam/pyterrier_dr/blob/master/pyterrier_dr/flex/np_retr.py)). Earlier years look the same. Keller, Breuer and Schaer's 2023 E5 runs used "a Faiss flat index" ([Keller et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10549)). AGH IR in 2025 discussed HNSW and FAISS as background, yet implemented cosine search over NumPy-cached vectors ([AGH IR 2025](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4038/paper_276.pdf)). The 2024 extended overview contains none of the words faiss, hnsw, approximate, nearest or dense ([LongEval 2024 overview](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3740/paper-213.pdf)).

The organisers' own explanations of drift leave no slot for the index. The 2026 overview says effectiveness on the DCTR click qrels falls over time "for almost all systems" while "the collection size increases, which might explain this observation", and that temporal robustness "does not seem to depend on any particular approach class" ([paper374](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)). The 2025 overview points to "decreasing overlap" in the document distribution ([LongEval 2025 overview](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17469v1)). The closest methodological ancestor, **Keller, Breuer and Schaer's ICTIR 2024 paper, decomposes temporal change by which test-collection component changes, using a CRUD view of documents, topics and qrels, but holds the system fixed and uses a BM25 first stage throughout** ([Evaluation of Temporal Change in IR Test Collections](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01373)). The replicability measures LongEval uses (Result Delta, RI, ΔRI, Effect Ratio, ARP) all compare one system across environments or against a pivot such as BM25 ([Keller et al., CLEF 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.05417)). No published work found uses exact search as the pivot. That is a clean, citable extension: the proposal adds a *system-internal* component to Keller et al.'s environment-side decomposition.

Two 2026 papers come close and must be cited and distinguished. Team Lexora reused the organisers' pre-built per-snapshot dense indexes, listed "online dense index updates" as an unassessed limitation, and named incremental dense index construction as future work ([Kumaran D et al., paper 379](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper379.pdf)). Their claim that the dense index "cannot adapt to newly added documents in subsequent snapshots" conflicts with their own setup, which loads a separate index for each snapshot. That supports the argument that index effects have not been studied carefully, but the paper should be cited for the open question, not for its explanation. Team GLHF proposed a weekly index-refresh trigger based on ingestion velocity, staleness and coverage gap, cited streaming IVF maintenance, and explicitly called it "scheduling/monitoring analysis, not … a causal claim" about effectiveness ([Wu and Yang, paper 384](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper384.pdf); [arXiv 2607.04088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04088)). The proposal differs from both because it builds an ANN index, varies its maintenance, and measures effectiveness against exact search. The DS@GT 2025 paper is the obvious hook for a DS@GT supervisor. Miyaguchi, Afrulbasha and Pramov ran sparse systems only, named dense retrieval as future work at "about a day of Nvidia v100 GPU-time" per date split, and suggested analysing "the semantic k-NN graph of a dense embedding model … in the context of the temporal evolution of systems" ([DS@GT at LongEval 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.08360)). The proposal follows directly from that sentence and should say so.

On the static side, the index has been measured but never longitudinally. **Lin's controlled Lucene comparison found HNSW negligible below 100K documents, "mostly small" between 100K and 1M, and measurable above 1M: on BioASQ (14.9M documents), HNSW averaged about 0.015 nDCG@10 below flat across five builds (range 0.011 to 0.020), and int8 HNSW about 0.017 below (maximum 0.024), roughly 4 to 5.8% relative** ([Lin, Operational Advice](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06464v1)). Macdonald and Tonellotto studied how ColBERT's FAISS candidate stage affects final effectiveness on a static collection ([Macdonald and Tonellotto, CIKM 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.11480)). A decision framework for dense retrieval reported both exact and HNSW variants and found HNSW Pareto-dominant on nDCG@10 against latency ([Are We There Yet?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.12993)). RAG studies find that **a search recall@10 of 0.7 costs only 2 to 3% gold-document recall relative to exhaustive search** ([Leto et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07396v1)), and that BERTScore moves at most 1.2% as Recall@k falls from 1.0 to 0.4 ([Dimitropoulos and Mamoulis](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04522)). All are static. None has a time axis.

## Four premises in the draft collide with published evidence

The first and most important collision is the problem statement itself. The draft says "evaluations usually treat this approximation as exact" and that index error is currently booked as "the model got worse". In LongEval the reverse holds. The dense runs are exact by construction, so **there is no existing misattribution in LongEval to correct. The real problem is that LongEval measures an idealised system nobody deploys at scale**, and that production teams watching their own drift cannot tell index error from model drift. The one place where hidden ANN error might already sit in a LongEval result is first-stage ColBERT. Keller et al. ran it through `pyterrier_colbert` and found it "impairs the performance further" ([Keller et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10549)), and end-to-end ColBERT uses a FAISS candidate stage ([Macdonald and Tonellotto](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.11480)). Whether that run used approximate candidate generation is unverified, so it is a hypothesis worth one sentence, not a claim.

The second collision is "there is little evidence on when [rebuilding] is actually needed". Systems research has studied exactly this. FreshDiskANN showed that "simple graph modification rules for insertions and deletions do not retain the same graph quality", with HNSW falling from about 98% to about 85% recall over 20 delete-reinsert cycles on SIFT1M (figure readings, approximate), and that merging a 10% change costs about 10% of a billion-scale rebuild, a "5-10x reduction in the cost of maintaining freshness" ([FreshDiskANN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.09613)). SPFresh found that a DiskANN global-rebuild schedule produced P99.9 latency spikes above 20 ms and declining recall, while local rebalancing held about 4 ms ([SPFresh](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14452v1)). Quake shows static Faiss-IVF and ScaNN "experience substantial performance decay over time" on a Wikipedia-12M workload that grows over 103 monthly snapshots, and fixes it with adaptive nprobe and cost-model-driven maintenance ([Quake](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03437v2)). Production systems use count-based triggers: **Milvus compacts when deleted rows pass 20% of total rows, and Qdrant vacuums at `deleted_threshold = 0.2`** ([Milvus](https://milvus.io/blog/2022-2-21-compact.md); [Qdrant](https://qdrant.tech/documentation/ops-optimization/optimizer/)). Most damaging for part (d), **a 2026 study found that signal-triggered repair does not beat fixed-cadence repair at matched budget, with every 95% confidence interval over four seeds including zero, and its authors withdrew their own v1 claim** ([When to Repair](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00728)). The defensible version: no study has measured the rebuild-versus-maintain trade-off on real evolving IR collections with relevance judgements, and no production system triggers maintenance from measured recall or quantizer drift. The draft's "expectation" that maintenance recovers most of the loss cheaply is already the systems literature's finding, so it should be framed as a test of whether that finding transfers to IR effectiveness, not as a hypothesis of the author's own.

The third collision is the compressed-vector mechanism. **DeDrift measured real temporal drift on VideoAds and YFCC and found PQ codecs lose under 1% recall in exhaustive search, while the IVF coarse quantizer loses 5 to 11 points as clusters unbalance** ([DeDrift](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.02752)). A 2026 preprint, not peer reviewed, found per-batch PQ retraining "statistically indistinguishable from no retraining in 8 of 9 cells", and argued streaming PQ loss is mostly a capacity problem ([IVF-TQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.17415v2)). Online PQ (2018) and CoDEQ (2025) already address codebook staleness, and data-oblivious methods such as RaBitQ avoid it by design ([Online PQ](https://arxiv.com/abs/1711.10775); [CoDEQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18335); [RaBitQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12497)). The draft's specific wording, vectors falling "outside those ranges", is in fact a description of scalar quantization with fitted per-dimension min/max or percentile ranges, and **no source found measures recall loss from stale SQ ranges under drift**. That is the narrow gap worth claiming, stated as a hypothesis to test. A reviewer will ask "why not RaBitQ?", so a data-oblivious arm belongs in the design.

The fourth collision is the query-drift signal. The ratio's ingredients are standard. k-th nearest-neighbour distance is a well-known label-free OOD score whose threshold "is estimated on ID data only" ([Sun et al., ICML 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06507v3)). **RoarGraph reports that OOD queries are 2.1x, 5.3x and 11.3x farther from their nearest neighbours than in-distribution queries**, and that in HNSW on LAION OOD queries need more than 500 hops against 48 to reach recall@10 of 0.93 ([RoarGraph](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.08933v1)). VIBE uses relative contrast and Mahalanobis distance distributions to characterise OOD queries and shows in-distribution benchmarks overstate OOD performance ([VIBE](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17810v1)). DARTH's recall predictor already uses query-side NN distances as its most important features and stays near its declared recall target on OOD T2I-100M queries ([DARTH](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19001v1)). No source found defines median(query-to-NN) over median(row-to-NN) as a run-time index-health alarm, so the operational use is new, but the statistic is not. The draft's "I would test whether recalibrating on recent queries brings recall back" also overlaps with Quake's adaptive scanning and DARTH, which adjust effort per query without labels.

A fifth claim needs softening rather than retraction. "On a fixed collection … a well-configured index loses very little" holds below 1M documents, but LongEval collections are larger. 2025 Sci had about 2M documents in its first snapshot, and 2025 Web had 14M test documents ([LongEval 2025 overview](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17469v1)), which is exactly the range where Lin measured 0.011 to 0.020 nDCG@10 losses ([Lin](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06464v1)). That cuts in the proposal's favour. The table below gives replacement wording for each contested claim.

| Draft claim | What the literature says | Recommended wording |

| "Well-configured index loses very little" | ~0.015 nDCG@10 at 14.9M docs | "Losses are negligible below 1M documents and measurable above, the scale of LongEval snapshots" |
| Graph "never designed around" new documents | Evidence is for deletes; append-only growth effect not isolated | "Deletions are known to damage HNSW reachability; whether time-ordered growth alone does is untested" |

## The decomposition is an identity, not a causal split

The method's arithmetic is correct and trivially so. With E_t as exact-search effectiveness and A_t as approximate effectiveness at snapshot t, the observed drop splits as A_0 − A_t = (E_0 − E_t) + (I_t − I_0), where I_t = E_t − A_t (a construction proposed in the notes, not taken from the literature). The split always holds, so it is accounting, not inference, and the draft should present it that way. **The index term is an interaction, not an independent additive component.** ANN loss depends on corpus size, intrinsic dimension and query distribution, all of which change between snapshots. The claim worth testing is that I_t changes over time and that maintenance changes it, not that I_t is "the index's fault" in isolation.

Corpus growth alone confounds the index term. A freshly rebuilt index at a fixed search budget loses recall as the collection grows: at fixed recall, distance computations scale as a power of N whose exponent depends on recall, intrinsic dimension, M and efConstruction ([Maghrebi et al., Power Law](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02143)). Practitioners report the same effect for HNSW behind RAG systems (not peer reviewed; [Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/hnsw-at-scale-why-your-rag-system-gets-worse-as-the-vector-database-grows/)). A growing I_t under "rebuild each snapshot" would therefore show size effects, not drift. The design needs a **size-matched control**: rebuild on a random subsample of snapshot t equal in size to snapshot 0, so growth and content drift separate.

HNSW raises a further subtlety the draft misses. **HNSW construction is already incremental insertion, so "rebuilt from scratch" and "updated incrementally" differ only in insertion order and in how deletions are handled.** For an append-only collection, an incrementally updated HNSW is simply an HNSW built in time order. The notes found no source that isolates append-only growth drift for HNSW. Insertion order alone moved recall by up to 5.6 to 7.1 points in hnswlib and 11.5 to 12.8 in FAISS in one study, though those figures partly come from a search summary ([Elliott and Clark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17813)). The damage the literature documents comes from deletions: hnswlib's replace-update repair leaves 3 to 4% of SIFT1M unreachable after 3,000 cycles of deleting and reinserting 5%, costing about 3% recall that "persists regardless of search parameter tuning" ([Xiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07871)). The proposal should separate three mechanisms: insertion order (time-ordered versus shuffled build on the same snapshot), deletions (tombstones versus `replace_deleted`, per the [hnswlib README](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib)), and distribution shift. Xiao's result also yields a sharp prediction: raising efSearch recovers loss caused by growth and query shift but not loss caused by unreachable nodes. That split is testable and would make the maintenance comparison mechanistic rather than descriptive.

Whether LongEval snapshots include deletions decides which mechanism the study can exercise. Web snapshots lose documents over time, since overlap "is highest between consecutive months and decreases over time" ([LongEval 2025 overview](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17469v1)). The 2026 overview reports the CORE collection growing ([paper374](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)), but the notes did not verify whether documents also leave it. Check this before committing to the 2026 Sci data alone. If CORE is append-only, the graph-repair arm has little to repair.

The IVF partition question deserves an arm even though the draft centres on HNSW. DeDrift's 5 to 11 point IVF losses are the largest drift effect in the quantization literature ([DeDrift](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.02752)), and Big-ANN's corrected 2023 streaming results showed the DiskANN baseline falling from a first-snapshot 0.883 to 0.7218 averaged over the clustered runbook ([Big-ANN 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17424v1)). An IVF arm gives the study a mechanism with a known non-zero effect, which protects against a null HNSW result.

## Sparse click qrels and unjudged neighbours bias the index effect toward zero

The measurement threat that matters most is specific to this design. Exact and approximate runs differ mostly on near-tie documents. In LongEval those documents are less likely to have been shown or clicked under the production ranker that generated the click-model qrels, so they tend to be unjudged and count as non-relevant (an inference from how the qrels are built). **The measured nDCG index effect is therefore biased toward zero, or toward an arbitrary sign.** Fröbe et al. show that trec_eval's default treatment of unjudged documents "favors systems that retrieve many (relevant) judged documents", that condensed lists "often overestimate" effectiveness, and that bootstrapped nDCG is more accurate ([Fröbe et al., ECIR 2023](https://downloads.webis.de/publications/papers/froebe_2023a.pdf)). BEIR found 14.4% to 31.8% of dense retrievers' top-10 results unjudged ([BEIR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08663)). Judgements are thin too: 2023 click qrels averaged 14 per topic with about one highly relevant document ([Keller et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.10549)). The mitigation is to report judged@10 for both runs and compute the effect under three treatments (unjudged as non-relevant, condensed, bootstrapped). The 2026 lab also released synthetic GPT-OSS-120b qrels alongside the DCTR click qrels ([paper374](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)), which give a second, denser judgement source. LLM judging of the specific documents where exact and approximate runs disagree would be cheap and would target the bias directly. No study yet applies bootstrapped or condensed nDCG to exact-versus-ANN comparisons, which is a small methodological contribution in its own right.

Statistical power is the second threat. At 50 topics, power to detect a 0.01 nDCG difference is very low. One summary of Urbano et al.'s simulations puts it near 10 to 15%, a figure to confirm in the paper's tables ([Urbano et al., SIGIR 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.11096)), and Sakai shows typical topic sets are too small for small effects ([Sakai 2016](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10791-015-9273-z.pdf)). The design helps here. Exact and approximate runs are paired on identical queries and qrels, so per-query paired t-tests or permutation tests (the two Urbano recommends) remove most qrel noise. The 2025 Web task offers 63,416 test queries ([LongEval 2025 overview](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17469v1)), while 2025 Sci had only 984. The 2026 per-snapshot query counts were not verified and should be checked before choosing the primary dataset.

Recall against exact search is the right qrel-free metric but the wrong sole metric. The draft says it "isolates the index's effect cleanly". It is immune to qrel noise, but **Recall@k predicts downstream quality with about 30% mean absolute deviation, against 0.5 to 2.6% for the inverse approximation ratio** ([Dimitropoulos and Mamoulis](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04522)), because ANN misses are usually near-ties. Report recall, a distance-ratio measure and nDCG together, and show per-query distributions, not only means. Average recall hides fragile queries: on GloVe-100, pgvector's overall recall of 0.87 concealed clusters at 0.21 against 0.99 ([RCheck](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25185)), and graph indexes show more skewed per-query recall than partition indexes ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.00379)). OOD queries are the documented locus of graph-index failure ([Steiner-hardness](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p4668-wang.pdf)), but no study links per-query ANN recall to per-query IR difficulty. Measuring that correlation would show whether index error is noise or a bias that hits the hard queries LongEval cares about.

Build non-determinism is the third threat. Lin's five BioASQ builds varied from 0.011 to 0.020 nDCG@10 loss ([Lin](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06464v1)), and the WSDM 2024 Lucene paper warns that "effectiveness figures may vary slightly from run to run" ([Lin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14963)). A single build per arm per snapshot cannot separate drift from build noise, so at least three to five seeds per arm are needed. The fourth threat is the null result. If the index effect is near zero at a generous setting, the study still yields findings only if the effect is reported as a curve. Sweep the search budget to fix recall-against-exact at about 0.90, 0.95 and 0.99, and include production-realistic int8 settings. The question then becomes "at which operating point does index drift become visible", which cannot come out empty.

The query-drift ratio has its own confounds. Its denominator, the row-to-NN distance, shrinks mechanically as the corpus densifies, so the ratio rises with growth even when queries do not move. Text queries are also asymmetric to documents (short query against long abstract), so the ratio exceeds one at baseline and only its change carries information. Near-duplicate documents drive row-to-NN distances toward zero; the Power Law paper had to de-duplicate a dataset that "contains many duplicate points" ([Power Law](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02143)). The clean test holds the corpus fixed and compares old and new queries against the same index, de-duplicates or uses the k-th rather than first neighbour, and reports how well the ratio predicts the measured recall gap. Steiner-hardness found distance-based measures correlate only moderately with graph search effort ([Steiner-hardness](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p4668-wang.pdf)), so the ratio must beat baselines (KNN OOD score, relative contrast, DARTH-style features), not merely move. Temporal precedence, "rises before quality visibly drops", needs many snapshots. Three in 2026 Sci cannot show it. The 2025 Web task's seven monthly test snapshots plus training months come closer, and Quake's 103-snapshot Wikipedia-12M workload ([Quake](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03437v2)) can serve as a qrel-free stress test. Calibrating on "recent queries" must use the previous snapshot's queries, never the test snapshot's, or the recovery result leaks.

Feasibility is manageable but not free. Exact references already exist: the organisers publish per-snapshot exact Qwen3 indexes on HuggingFace ([baseline README](https://github.com/clef-longeval/longeval-code/tree/main/clef26/scientific-retrieval/baseline-pyterrier-dense)), and pyterrier-dr offers `faiss_hnsw_retriever()` as a drop-in approximate counterpart ([pyterrier-dr](https://github.com/terrierteam/pyterrier_dr)). Lucene flat search on FEVER's 5.4M documents ran at 7.3 QPS on a 20-core machine ([Lin](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06464v1)), so CPU exact search over tens of thousands of queries takes hours per snapshot per configuration, and GPU brute force is the standard route at scale ([Johnson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)). Re-embedding is the real cost, about a V100-day per Web split for DS@GT ([DS@GT 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.08360)), so reuse the released 2026 embeddings and keep the encoder fixed across snapshots. Model updates become a separate, optional arm.

## Recommended changes to framing, questions and method

The framing should move from "the index is a hidden variable LongEval gets wrong" to "longitudinal IR evaluation measures an idealised exact system; this study measures what the deployed approximate index adds over time, and what each maintenance action recovers". That claim is true on the evidence, survives a supervisor who has read the LongEval code, and keeps the practical motivation. Cite DS@GT 2025's k-NN-graph suggestion as the origin, Keller et al.'s ICTIR 2024 decomposition as the evaluation ancestor, and FreshDiskANN, SPFresh, Quake and DeDrift as the systems ancestors whose synthetic results this study tests on real data.

The research questions should be sharpened as follows. RQ1 becomes: at fixed recall-against-exact operating points (0.90, 0.95, 0.99), how large is I_t on nDCG under click and LLM qrels and on recall and distance ratio, how does it change across snapshots, and how much of the change survives a size-matched rebuild control? RQ2 becomes: which maintenance action recovers which share of the loss, namely search-budget recalibration (growth and query shift), graph repair (deletions and unreachable nodes), and scalar-quantization range refitting (clipping), at what compute and memory cost against a full rebuild, and does any drift-triggered policy beat a fixed cadence and a 20%-deleted count trigger at matched budget, the bar set by [When to Repair](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00728)? RQ3 becomes: does a size-controlled, label-free query-to-corpus NN-distance statistic predict the recall gap on new queries better than established OOD and hardness measures, and does calibrating on the previous snapshot's queries close that gap? Guidance (d) then becomes a decision rule evaluated against an oracle, rather than advice.

The arms table should grow from five setups to a factorial that isolates mechanisms. Keep exact search, rebuild per snapshot, incremental without maintenance, incremental with maintenance, and frozen first-snapshot settings. Add a size-matched rebuild, a time-ordered versus shuffled build on the same snapshot, tombstone versus `replace_deleted` deletions where the data has removals, each maintenance lever applied alone and then combined, an IVF arm where drift effects are documented, and a data-oblivious quantizer arm (RaBitQ-style) to answer the obvious reviewer question. Run three to five seeds per arm, test per query with paired t-tests or permutation tests, and report judged@10 together with nDCG under the unjudged-as-non-relevant, condensed and bootstrapped treatments. Use exact search as the pivot in RI, ΔRI and ER, so the results speak LongEval's own language.

The data plan needs one check and one addition. Verify the 2026 snapshot sizes, query counts and whether CORE drops documents, none of which the notes confirmed. Add the 2025 Web task (seven monthly snapshots, document removals, 63K test queries) for horizon and power, and Quake's Wikipedia-12M for a long qrel-free series. Several numbers in this report are figure readings or summaries, notably FreshDiskANN's recall curves, the Urbano power figure and the Elliott and Clark order effects, and should be read off the PDFs before they appear in the proposal. One fetch summary in the research hallucinated FAISS and HNSW in the 2024 overview, which full-text search disproved, so every "LongEval never did X" sentence should rest on the full-text checks cited here. The "not found" findings rest on about 15 to 20 searches per topic, not a systematic review, and the LSM-style vector indexes (LSM-VEC, Greator) were not examined.

## Conclusion

The proposal's best asset is the one it undersells. LongEval has spent four years explaining drift with a system that is exact by construction, while the systems community has spent five years measuring index decay with no relevance judgements at all. The contribution is to join those two literatures on real data, and it holds whether the index effect turns out large or small. A large effect means LongEval's robustness conclusions do not transfer to deployed systems. A small one, shown across operating points, gives the lab a measured licence to keep ignoring the index. Either answer gets published, provided the effect is reported as a curve with size controls, not as a single number at a generous setting.

The sharper scientific bet sits in RQ2's mechanism split, not in the decomposition. If search-budget recalibration recovers growth and query-shift loss but not deletion damage, and SQ range refitting matters where PQ refitting does not, the study produces a maintenance taxonomy that neither IR nor systems research has. The hardest bar is the 2026 null on repair timing: a drift-triggered policy has to beat a fixed cadence at matched cost, and the honest prior is that it will not. Building the guidance around that possibility from the start, so that "rebuild on a fixed schedule, recalibrate the search budget every snapshot" is an acceptable answer, is what makes the proposal bulletproof.
