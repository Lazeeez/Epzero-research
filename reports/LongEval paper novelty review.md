# Claim the longitudinal join, concede the components

**Claim (1) survives the prior-work search as novel. No paper found tracks the ANN-minus-exact nDCG@10 gap across snapshots of a relevance-judged longitudinal collection. Claims (3), (4), (5) and (6) are novel but narrow: each applies a known phenomenon or method to a setting nobody has measured. Claim (2) is known by construction and belongs in the method section, not the contribution list.** The static size of the gap is well documented. Lin measured HNSW at 0.000–0.015 nDCG@10 below flat search on BEIR and already remarked that "retrieval quality degrades more as corpus size increases", though across different corpora rather than within one growing collection ([Lin, ACL 2025 Industry](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06464)). Insertion order is known to move HNSW recall by up to 12.8 points ([Elliott and Clark](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.17813v1)). Condensed-list nDCG′ dates from 2007 ([Sakai, SIGIR 2007](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1277741.1277756)). Query-driven graph repair has a SIGMOD 2026 paper reporting the opposite of claim (6) in a different regime ([NGFix/RFix](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2510.22316)). What none of this work does is join the two literatures. Systems papers track index decay over time, but only as neighbour recall with no relevance judgments. IR papers track effectiveness over time, but with exact search or with the index held fixed. The LongEval 2026 working notes state this gap outright ("we cannot assess the impact of online dense index updates"; [Kumaran D et al.](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper379.pdf)). The paper should therefore lead with the join, hedge every "first" with "to our knowledge", and name the closest prior work inside each claim rather than in a separate related-work paragraph. Four pieces of work would most strengthen it against review: an LLM fill-in of the holes created by ANN substitution, a size-matched subsample control for growth, an equivalence test across the three seeds, and a specific explanation of why NGFix's published gains do not appear in this setting.

### Claim (1): the time axis is the contribution, not the magnitude

The static magnitudes match prior work almost exactly, so a reviewer will treat them as known. Lin's controlled Lucene study (BGE on BEIR, M=16, efSearch=1000, five trials) found HNSW-minus-flat nDCG@10 between 0.000 and 0.015, with BioASQ worst at 0.015 (range 0.011–0.020). Lin noted that "retrieval quality degrades more as corpus size increases" and that HNSW construction is non-deterministic ([Lin, arXiv 2409.06464](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06464)). The Anserini HNSW integration reported an efSearch sweep on MS MARCO, with MRR@10 at 0.2551/0.3612/0.3842 for efSearch 10/100/1000 against 0.390 for flat, and a BEIR average of 0.415 against 0.417 ([Anserini HNSW, arXiv 2304.12139](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.12139)). The paper's "< 0.012 at ~95% recall" therefore confirms these numbers on a new collection. Presented that way, it strengthens the paper. What no source does is follow this quantity across snapshots of one judged collection. The nearest temporal work is **PLAID SHIRTTT**: centroids built on the first 5% of a date-ordered NeuCLIR stream give nDCG@20 of 0.047, against 0.440 with all of it ([Lawrie et al., SIGIR 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00975)). But it concerns ColBERT/PLAID late interaction, compares against an oracle-centroid PLAID index rather than exact search, and has no history or shuffle control. Systems work tracks drift over time in neighbour recall only ([DeDrift](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02752); [Quake](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03437v2)). Inside LongEval, the 2026 overview names no index type for the Qwen3-4B baseline ([LongEval 2026 overview](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)). An earlier check of the organisers' run metadata found pyterrier-dr's exhaustive `NumpyRetriever` ([baseline ir-metadata](https://github.com/clef-longeval/longeval-code/blob/main/clef26/scientific-retrieval/baseline-pyterrier-dense/baseline-qwen3-embedding-4b/ir-metadata.yml)). So LongEval's published temporal findings describe exact search. One unverified preprint is the most likely hidden overlap. "As We May Search" reportedly shows exact dense nDCG@10 falling from 0.990 at 1K documents to 0.789 at 500K, and HNSW losing about 2% against flat at 1M, on personal-scale corpora. It is known only from a search snippet and must be read before submission ([arXiv 2606.29652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29652)).

### Claim (2): true by construction, valuable as a control

No paper was found that states or tests bit-identity between an incrementally maintained HNSW and a rebuild from the same history. The reason is that standard HNSW construction is itself repeated insertion: "HNSW sequentially adds new vectors to the index … finds neighbors of the new data by performing ANNS on the index under construction" ([PVLDB 18, p1825](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol18/p1825-liu.pdf)). The property holds only under a fixed level-RNG seed and single-threaded insertion. Threaded hnswlib builds give "different result & accuracy every time" ([hnswlib #28](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib/issues/28)), and ParlayANN exists precisely to make parallel builds deterministic ([ParlayANN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.04359v2.pdf)). For IVF with fixed, pre-trained centroids, incremental insertion is assignment only. Deployed engines rarely keep one incremental graph. Lucene/Elasticsearch merges segment graphs by reinserting vectors, with the acknowledged "risk … that we damage the graph quality" ([Elastic Search Labs](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/hnsw-graphs-speed-up-merging)). The paper should present (2) as a verified precondition. Its job is to let the paper say that any incremental-versus-rebuild difference is purely an insertion-order effect.

### Claim (3): age-conditioned misses are unmeasured, but the mechanism story must be careful

Reviewers will cite Elliott and Clark for "order matters". Their study, however, measures *aggregate* recall@10 under LID-sorted, random and category-sequential orders (up to 12.8 points on FAISS at efSearch=10). It contains no per-point or per-insertion-position analysis and no test of whether later inserts are retrieved less ([Elliott and Clark, arXiv 2405.17813](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.17813v1)). Their category-sequential order is the closest analogue of snapshot-sequential arrival. Their finding that the effect shrinks from efSearch 10 to 40 supports the paper's "wider search, not reordering" result rather than contradicting it. Two other papers constrain the mechanism the paper may claim. The hub paper tested whether early-inserted nodes become hubs and found insertion order explains only about 0.1–0.5% of variance in node access counts on real data ([Munyampirwa et al., arXiv 2412.01940](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.01940v3)). The unreachable-points literature attributes in-degree-0 nodes to delete/replace repair and calls their formation from construction alone "exceedingly small" ([Xiao et al., arXiv 2407.07871](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07871v2)). Under append-only growth, the paper should therefore describe the excess as *soft under-connection from temporally clustered arrival*, sensitive to ef. It should not claim hard unreachability or hub formation by age. For IVF, the ~1.15x excess fits the stale-centroid story documented by DeDrift and Ada-IVF ([DeDrift](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02752); [Ada-IVF](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00970v1)), not an order effect. With fixed centroids, IVF assignment does not depend on order. No study was found measuring whether ANN indexes retrieve newer items less often at equal exact rank. The age-conditioned measurement and the new-content-query result are the novel core. The IR framing reviewers will expect is index-induced, time-conditioned *retrievability*, after Azzopardi and Vinay (CIKM 2008; details not re-verified).

### Claim (4): the curve shape and the calibration-power point are new; "it depends on recall" is not

Recent work agrees that neighbour recall is a weak proxy for downstream quality and that the curve is *flat*. At search recall 0.95, gold-document recall@10 falls by only about 0.004–0.010, and recall 0.7 costs "only … a 2-3% drop" ([Leto et al., arXiv 2411.07396](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07396)). Separately, recall can fall 60% while BERTScore moves at most 1.2% ([Dimitropoulos and Mamoulis, arXiv 2606.04522](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04522v1)). Both are static, use QA or RAG corpora of at most 79K items (Dimitropoulos and Mamoulis), and span a recall axis of 0.4–1.0. The paper's finding is that judged nDCG@10 is a *steep* function of recall within the 0.9–1.0 region and that it steepens as the collection grows. That is a real contrast worth stating explicitly. No paper reporting nDCG@10 on TREC-style qrels as a function of measured neighbour recall at 95% and 98% operating points was found. On calibration, Quake states that "LIRE's recall degrades over time as it uses a static nprobe" ([Quake](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03437v2)). Ada-ef measured a stale calibration falling to 0.945 average recall against a 0.95 target after 50% insertions, with P1 recall at 0.720 ([Ada-ef, arXiv 2512.06636](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06636v1)). So "fixed parameters lose recall under growth" is known. What appears new is the *resolution* argument: a 50-query, doubling-grid calibration lacks the statistical power to detect a one-point drift. Fifty queries is far below every calibration set in the literature. Ada-ef samples 200 data vectors, DARTH uses 10K learning queries, and NGFix uses 1M–10M historical queries.

### Claim (5): a textbook method applied where nobody has applied it

Condensed lists, which drop unjudged documents before scoring, are Sakai's ([SIGIR 2007](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1277741.1277756)). Sakai's own follow-up found that condensed-list metrics "are not necessarily superior … for handling biases" ([Sakai, IMT 2009](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/imt/4/2/4_2_547/_article/-char/en)). Sakai and Kando reportedly found that they overestimate systems whose holes are truly non-relevant (content from memory; [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10791-008-9059-7)). BEIR already showed that dense runs carry heavy holes against lexically biased pools. On TREC-COVID, Hole@10 was 30.6% for DPR and 31.8% for TAS-B, and manual re-annotation raised ANCE from 0.654 to 0.735 nDCG@10 ([BEIR, arXiv 2104.08663](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08663)). LLM hole-filling restores Kendall τ to 0.92–0.93 when 90% of judgments are removed, against 0.51–0.72 when holes count as non-relevant ([Upadhyay, Kamalloo and Lin, arXiv 2405.04727](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2405.04727)). No paper was found that measures hole rates or condensed-list effects for ANN-versus-exact runs of the same encoder. ANN-in-IR papers report the small nDCG drop on existing qrels as real loss ([Hofstätter et al., arXiv 2206.12993](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.12993); [Anserini HNSW](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.12139)). The setting is unusually clean: the ANN run differs from a pooled exact run *only* on its substitutions, so the pooling artefact falls directly on the measurement of the efficiency trade-off.

### Claim (6): a negative result that must explain a published positive one

NGFix/RFix is the most dangerous prior work in the whole review. Its authors report QPS–recall and NDC–rderr curves on which repaired HNSW beats plain HNSW across the ef sweep. They explicitly reject wider search: "A common and simple approach … is to increase the search list size, but this will lead to an increase in search latency" ([NGFix, PACMMOD 3(6) / SIGMOD 2026](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2510.22316)). Its regime differs from the paper's on several axes. It uses about 1M–10M historical queries with brute-force exact kNN, cross-modal out-of-distribution data, the rderr metric rather than recall in the NDC plots, matched QPS rather than matched NDC against HNSW, and up to 48 extra out-edges per node. The paper should state these differences explicitly. RoarGraph and OOD-DiskANN also report wins for query-aware construction on OOD queries ([RoarGraph, PVLDB 17](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p2735-chen.pdf); [OOD-DiskANN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12850)). EnhanceGraph adds edges from search-log local optima, close to witness edges. It reports large Recall@1 gains (GIST1M 41.74% to 93.42%) and argues analytically that the overhead equals "increasing the search parameter L by 2", but it never runs a matched-L control ([EnhanceGraph, arXiv 2506.13144](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13144v2)). The methodological precedent is "When to Repair". At matched repair budget, triggered repair "does not" beat fixed cadence, and the v1 positive result came from an "interpolated-baseline trap" ([Mandarapu and Kunkunuru, arXiv 2607.00728](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00728)). That paper matches repair passes, not query distance work, and does not compare against wider ef. A matched-NDC, matched-recall comparison against the same graph searched wider was not found as a headline anywhere. The defensible claim is the negative result *in this regime*. Encoder-generated pseudo-query bridging has no precedent found. Frontier resumption is conceptually close to adaptive early termination ([DARTH](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19001v2); LAET, SIGMOD 2020, not fetched).

## Position the paper as a fourth component of temporal change

The cleanest positioning is a taxonomy of four literatures that never meet. The first covers test-collection-side temporal change: Keller, Breuer and Schaer decompose it into document, topic and qrel changes via CRUD operations and hold the system fixed ([Keller et al., ICTIR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01373)), alongside the LongEval overviews ([2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17469v1); [2026](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)) and "Still Fresh?" ([Kuissi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04532)). The second is encoder-side staleness: MURR ([arXiv 2504.10250](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10250)) and GradNormIR ([Findings of ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.1334.pdf)). The third is codebook staleness in late interaction (PLAID SHIRTTT). The fourth is ANN-structure maintenance measured only by neighbour recall: FreshDiskANN ([arXiv 2105.09613](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09613)), Ada-IVF, Quake, DeDrift and the Big-ANN streaming track ([arXiv 2409.17424](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17424v1)). The paper sits at the uncovered join: ANN-structure state and history, with the encoder fixed, measured by judged effectiveness against an exact-search control. Two conceptual anchors give it a lineage. Stahlberg and Byrne's search-error/model-error split, which uses exact search to expose what approximate search hides ([EMNLP 2019](https://aclanthology.org/D19-1331/)), supplies the method. Keller et al.'s component decomposition supplies the frame, to which the paper adds a system-internal, index-state component. Reimers and Gurevych are the classic citation for "growth hurts dense retrieval", for exact dense versus sparse search ([ACL 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-short.77)). The paper should cite them to separate the growth effect on exact search from the growth effect on the ANN gap.

Two LongEval 2026 sentences motivate the paper better than any argument the authors could write. Kumaran D et al. used the organisers' pre-built per-snapshot indexes "as provided" and wrote: "we cannot assess the impact of online dense index updates" ([paper 379](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper379.pdf)). Wu and Yang asked "when should a retrieval system be updated as the scientific collection continues to grow?", then treated update triggers "as a separate system-maintenance layer", citing Ada-IVF ([arXiv 2607.04088](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04088v1)). Their "reindexing" means including documents in the corpus, not ANN structure. The paper should quote both and say it closes that question.

## Conclusion

The six claims are not equally strong, and the paper is better if it says so. Claim (1) is the reason the paper exists. Claims (3) and (5) are its most interesting findings, because they turn two familiar facts, that insertion order matters and that pools have holes, into measurements that change how an IR researcher should read an ANN number. The late-snapshot miss excess means approximate search is biased against new content, not just noisier, and that bias concentrates on exactly the queries a longitudinal benchmark exists to test. The pooling result means part of the published ANN "loss" across IR is unmeasurable, not real. Together they support a sharper message than "the index barely matters": *at standard operating points the index barely moves the average, but it moves the wrong documents, and the qrels cannot see most of the movement.*

Claim (6) carries the most risk and the most value as a negative result. Its credibility depends on explaining NGFix rather than ignoring it. "When to Repair" shows that matched-budget negative results are publishable when the comparison is airtight and the regime is stated. Claim (2) should be demoted to method without hesitation: the bit-identity check is what makes the shuffled-order comparison clean, and that is enough. The pre-registered, hash-frozen protocol was not assessed for novelty in the notes. It should be presented as a strength of rigour, not as a claimed contribution.

## Addendum (20 November 2026): items verified after this review

Five of the open items above were checked against primary sources.

1. **"As We May Search"** (Zerhoudi, Roegiest, Mitrović and Granitzer; ICTIR 2026;
   <https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29652>) is a partial overlap and a must-cite, not a threat.
   - It compares flat, HNSW and IVF search (FAISS, all-MiniLM-L6-v2) on MS MARCO subsets, and its
     abstract reports HNSW "extending this to 1M with only 2% quality loss".
   - Its subsets are static and independently sampled. Its search settings are fixed, with no
     neighbour-recall control. It has no time axis, no index maintenance and no insertion order.
   - Its Table 3 numbers (flat/HNSW/IVF nDCG@10 of 0.789/0.777/0.763 at 500K and
     0.736/0.719/0.677 at 1M) were read through a summarizing tool, so confirm them in the PDF
     before quoting them.
2. **LongEval 2026 judgments** (extended overview, CEUR Vol-4283, paper 374). The LLM labels use
   the UMBRELA prompt on a **depth-10 pool of the 24 Task 1 systems**, with GPT-OSS-120B judging
   the query only. The official dense baseline is in that pool. This is why exact search is
   99.5% judged at depth 10 while approximate search's substitutes are not. The overview does
   not state the baseline's index type ("implemented with PyTerrier-Dense").
3. **Snapshot sizes.** The overview's introduction says "roughly 870K to 1.7M documents"; the
   "approximately 87K" in its Section 2 is a typo. The released vectors hold 869,902 documents.
4. **Slipstream** (Yang and Zhao, arXiv 2606.02992, June 2026) speeds up streaming HNSW insertion
   by reusing the previous insertion's candidates. It reports aggregate recall@10 only. It has no
   recall by insertion time and no comparison of insertion orders, so it does not overlap with
   claim 3. Its Sections 4.3 and 5 were read only in summary.
5. **A practitioner report in the opposite direction.** A Chroma issue
   (<https://github.com/chroma-core/chroma/issues/7963>, open) reports that a small group of
   vectors inserted *early* became poorly linked after a much larger batch arrived, and that
   inserting in the other order avoided it. It is a single unreviewed report. It shows that the
   direction of an insertion-order effect is not obvious, so claim 3 needs a measured mechanism:
   incoming links by arrival group, and a reversed-order build.

Two further results bear on the positioning. Both are in
`research/longeval/HELDOUT_EXPLORATORY.md`, sections 6 and 9:

- Our exact search reproduces the official `baseline-qwen3-4b` nDCG@10 to within 0.0014 on both
  label sets.
- The top 10 of exact search and of every primary run, pooled over seeds and snapshots, holds
  5,511 query–document pairs. The organisers never graded 445 of them, and 635 lack a grade at
  one or more of the snapshots where they were retrieved. Filling those holes with the
  organisers' own judge model is a small job.
