# Graph indexes shortchange late arrivals; averages hide it

The core of the claim is new and holds up, but the universal version does not. Nobody has published a per-item, age-stratified measurement of retrieval loss in an ANN index. No streaming benchmark, vendor test or IR study reports recall split by when an item arrived. Our unpublished LongEval-Sci 2026 measurements therefore fill a real gap. They show HNSW dropping relevant new documents 2–3x as often as old ones (pooled), while the average ANN-vs-exact nDCG@10 gap barely moves (|Δ| < 0.006). The mechanism, however, is not "staleness" in the sense of structures fitted to an old data distribution. It is **insertion-position history-dependence** in incrementally built graphs. The base-layer in-degree gradient is identical in shuffled builds. The cohort disparity vanishes when the build order is shuffled (1.00–1.03) and reverses when the order is reversed (0.82). Our own frozen-IVF arm shows no disparity at all (0.99 pooled), which agrees with the peer-reviewed IVF literature: partition and quantizer staleness needs real distribution drift and mostly shows up as cost, not as systematic misses of new items. The defensible claim is therefore narrower and sharper. Every index family's chance of returning an item depends on index history, not only on geometry. In incremental graphs this produces an arrival-time bias even without any drift. In partition and quantizer indexes it appears only under drift. Average recall cannot detect either. That framing fits LongEval's temporal-persistence question directly, it is open in the literature as of November 2026, and it is at real risk of being taken within months, given at least five active threads circling the same mechanism.

## Newcomer isolation was named, never measured

The graph-index literature has every ingredient of the finding except the measurement. GRAB-ANNS (a March 2026 preprint) names the mechanism almost word for word. Under its "Isolation of Newcomers", existing neighbours form a "geometric shield" that makes new nodes fail the RNG diversity test, leaving them "under-connected (isolated) and thus unreachable". It **gives no per-node or per-age quantification**. It evaluates only on GPU, with batch insertion and fixed degree, on filtered SIFT-128, reporting aggregate filtered recall against its freshness discount α ([arXiv 2604.16402](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16402)). A Lucene committer opened issue #15427 in November 2025. It asks whether early vectors have poor neighbours "since they didn't see all the future vectors yet" and proposes plotting neighbour quality against insertion time, but no data was ever posted. A 2026-11-17 comment calls the problem "future-blindness" ([apache/lucene#15427](https://github.com/apache/lucene/issues/15427)). The hnswlib shuffle PR states the asymmetry informally: an element "can acquire a later element only if that element subsequently selects it", and the heuristic "is not symmetric, so often it does not" ([nmslib/hnswlib#674](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib/pull/674)).

The quantitative precedents measure neighbouring quantities, never this one. Elliott and Clark (peer-reviewed, ICTIR '24) show that *whole-index* recall moves by up to **12.8 points** when the insertion order is sorted by local intrinsic dimensionality or category. They do not look at early versus late items, in-degree or reachability ([arXiv 2405.17813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17813)). That is a corpus-order effect tied to data properties; ours is a position effect that persists under random order. The FlatNav "hub highway" paper is the only published test of insertion order against a node-centrality measure. Insertion order explains **at most 0.5% of the variance in node access counts** on real data (GloVe, NYTimes, GIST, DEEP, SpaceV), and in-degree was never measured ([arXiv 2412.01940v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.01940v3)). The two results can both be true, because a halving of *mean* in-degree across deciles can sit alongside low node-level R² for traffic. A reviewer who knows FlatNav will still raise it, so the paper must explain the difference in what was measured. On the forward side, a PVLDB 2025 paper shows that node-by-node insertion caps candidate-neighbour quality at about **0.5**, because each insert sees only the partial graph ([arXiv 2410.01231v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01231v2)). Together with our in-degree gradient this gives a two-sided picture. Early nodes have weaker forward links but plenty of in-links; late nodes have good forward links but few in-links. No published model gives in-degree as a function of insertion time.

The "unreachable points" literature is a near miss in a different regime. MN-RU finds 3–4% of points with zero in-degree after about 3,000 delete-and-reinsert cycles on SIFT, with a ~3% recall loss that raising ef does not fix. It explicitly calls unreachable points during plain construction "exceedingly small" in likelihood ([arXiv 2407.07871](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.07871)). FreshDiskANN attributes recall decay under churn to "very aggressive pruning policies" ([arXiv 2105.09613](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09613)). Lucene's merge path loses **7.0 pp recall@100** after 50 generations of 2% deletes as out-degree decays to 15.5 against 34.7 for a rebuild ([apache/lucene#16552](https://github.com/apache/lucene/issues/16552)). Every one of these treats link loss as damage from deletes or merges. Our finding comes from insert-only growth, which this literature has framed as safe.

Hubness supplies the main confound to control for, not a precedent. Radovanović et al. established that k-occurrence skew is intrinsic to high-dimensional data ([JMLR](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/radovanovic10a.html)). A peer-reviewed SIGMOD 2026 study frames graph-ANN designs as hubness mitigations, describes "isolated anti-hubs" that break greedy routing, and finds a trade-off between suppressing hubs and keeping anti-hubs reachable ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3802120)). Anti-hub status there depends on position in *space*; our effect depends on position in the *insertion sequence*. Our research team could not read the full SIGMOD text. It is the single most important paper to read before claiming novelty, because it could contain in-degree-by-order plots.

The practitioner evidence is recent and degenerate but telling. In FAISS, with 200 identical copies among 1,000 vectors at M=16, **28/200 copies have in-degree 0**, because reverse links from later copies lose to tied earlier ones ([faiss#5968](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/issues/5968)). VSAG/seekdb has a sentinel vector that is never found after 4,000 duplicates ([seekdb#2653](https://github.com/oceanbase/seekdb/issues/2653)). In Chroma, freshly inserted vectors are missed by self-queries at rates that rise from 15% to about 63% as N grows, and M=48 makes it *worse* ([chroma#7998](https://github.com/chroma-core/chroma/issues/7998)). The duplicate cases are limiting instances of our mechanism: full older neighbours prune the newcomer away. Chroma #7963 runs in the opposite direction. A small set inserted *first* becomes hard to find after a large batch, with self-recall dropping to 80–109/120 against 115–118/120 when the large batch goes first ([chroma#7963](https://github.com/chroma-core/chroma/issues/7963)). Read alongside our observation that the bias is strongest (2.0–2.7) for new documents among mostly-old neighbours, this points to a general principle: **an item is disadvantaged when it is a temporal minority in its local neighbourhood**, whichever direction the minority runs. That is a stronger and more testable hypothesis than "new is worse". All of these GitHub reports are unreviewed and mostly unconfirmed by maintainers. They are practitioner signals, not results.

| Nearest work | What it shows | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| GRAB-ANNS (preprint, 2026) | Names newcomer isolation; freshness-discounted pruning | No age measurement; GPU batch only |
| Elliott & Clark (ICTIR '24) | Global recall depends on data-correlated order (≤12.8 pp) | No per-item or early/late analysis |
| FlatNav (preprint) | Insertion order explains ≤0.5% of access-count variance | In-degree, recall by age |
| Lucene #15427 (2025–26) | Hypothesises age-dependent neighbour quality | No data |
| MN-RU, FreshDiskANN, Lucene #16552 | Link loss and unreachability under churn | Insert-only growth |
| FAISS #5968, seekdb #2653 | Zero-in-degree newcomers with exact duplicates | Ordinary geometry, smooth gradient |

## Partition and quantizer staleness needs drift and shows up as cost first

The IVF literature broadly supports the view that structures fitted to past data go stale, but it does not support a universal new-item penalty. The strongest real-drift study is DeDrift (ICCV 2023). On 86M VideoAds and 84M YFCC vectors, a coarse quantizer trained on the first months loses up to **11 points of 10-recall@10 at tight budgets** (IVF16384: 0.842 vs 0.732 at 6,000 distance computations). The loss shrinks to about one point at generous budgets, and the PQ/OPQ codecs lose **under 0.011 absolute** ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.02752)). DeDrift's out-of-domain protocol is the harsh limit: the indexed data comes entirely from after training. In a growing collection the old half is in-domain by construction, so DeDrift's losses are an upper bound for our setting, not an estimate. HAKES (peer-reviewed, PVLDB 2025) holds out ImageNet categories and drifts both inserts and queries. It reports that recall "degrades slightly", which shows "the robustness of IVF ... against moderate drifts for embeddings from the same model" ([PVLDB PDF](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol18/p3049-ooi.pdf)). Ada-IVF concedes that frozen centroids are fine when the initial sample is large and the distribution stable, and its headline metric is QPS at fixed recall, where frozen centroids score 0.27–0.81 of a rebuild ([arXiv 2411.00970](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00970v1)). Quake's real Wikipedia-12M trace, which grows from 1.6M to 12M over 103 monthly updates, mostly shows staleness as latency. Unmaintained Faiss-IVF needed 165.8 hours of search time, and LIRE missed its recall target only because nprobe was static ([arXiv 2506.03437v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03437v2)).

Against that background, our frozen-IVF null (0.99 pooled, 0.87 within-query) is expected rather than anomalous, and it is useful. An IVF item's probe probability depends only on centroid geometry and the query, with no path dependence, so without strong drift a new item in a dense region is no worse off than an old one. **This null is the most important fact for the "all index families" question: our own data refutes the universal claim.**

Quantizers show only two plausible age-dependent failure modes. HAKES reports that applying search-side learned compression to new inserts gives true neighbours among the added data "significantly lower approximated similarity than those neighbors in the original dataset" ([PVLDB PDF](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol18/p3049-ooi.pdf)). This is the only published statement of a new-item disadvantage in a quantized index, and it is specific to learned compression. Global-range scalar quantization is the other mode. cuVS clips values outside a learned range ([cuVS docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuvs/user-guide/api-guides/preprocessing-guide/scalar-quantizer)), while Lucene recomputes per-segment quantiles on merge when they diverge ([Elastic blog](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/scalar-quantization-in-lucene)). Nobody has measured the clipping rate of late arrivals. That measurement is cheap and would be new. Contrary preprints exist. CoDEQ argues frozen PQ degrades under synthetic cluster-ordered streams ([arXiv 2512.18335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18335)). IVF-TQ claims −3.8 pp for IVF-PQ even under shuffled i.i.d. ingestion ([arXiv 2605.17415](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17415)). Both are unreplicated, and the IVF-TQ claim conflicts with DeDrift's peer-reviewed codec results.

Search-width calibration is the third leg of the original claim, and the evidence for it is real but only aggregate. PQR-Search (an ICDE 2027 submission, preprint) tunes indexes to 0.99 average recall and then applies five 10% cluster-ordered update steps with no retuning. The share of queries below target rises from 11.1% to 16.7% (hnswlib), 13.5% to 22.3% (IVF-Flat) and 7.9% to 17.5% (Quake), and Quake's worst query falls from 0.73 to 0.42. Notably, "graph indexes degraded gradually even under random insertion alone" (HNSW 21.0%→26.9% on SIFT, k=100), which fits a drift-free graph mechanism ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.07579)). Learned and conformal stopping rules (LAET, DARTH, CTR) all calibrate under exchangeability, which a growing collection breaks by construction. No one has measured how their calibration error grows with collection age ([arXiv 2607.02338](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.02338)).

## Every streaming benchmark averages away the (query, item) pair

No benchmark in use could detect our finding, even in principle. The NeurIPS'23 Big-ANN streaming track ranks entries on recall@10 averaged over all queries and checkpoints, against the live set at each checkpoint, on synthetic k-means-clustered runbooks ([neurips23 README](https://github.com/harsha-simhadri/big-ann-benchmarks/blob/main/neurips23/README.md)). Its own history shows how much a single scalar can hide. A caching bug reported first-snapshot recall instead of the runbook average. Puck, the declared winner, went from **0.985 to 0.0921** once the bug was fixed, and the winner changed ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.17424v1)). The per-checkpoint ground truth and each point's insertion step both exist in the repository, so per-age recall could be computed from it after the fact. The data exists; the metric does not. VIBE, now the community's successor to ANN-Benchmarks, has no streaming workloads at all. Its RC100 hardness slice stratifies *queries*, not indexed items ([arXiv HTML v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17810v3)).

The vendor and academic streaming tests share the same blind spot. Intel SVS, Cosmos DB DiskANN, CANDY, CANDOR-Bench, the NeurIPS 2025 deletion-evaluation workshop paper, Quake and CoDEQ all plot *average* recall per step ([SVS docs](https://intel.github.io/ScalableVectorSearch/benchs/dynamic.html); [Cosmos DB blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/cosmosdb/azure-cosmos-db-with-diskann-part-4-stable-vector-search-recall-with-streaming-data/); [arXiv 2512.06200](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06200)). VectorDBBench's streaming case appears to score against full-dataset ground truth: a vendor post notes a "theoretical max recall for this case is 0.80" at 80% write progress. If so, it cannot separate "not yet inserted" from "inserted but unreachable" ([OceanBase blog](https://en.oceanbase.com/blog/streaming-workload-benchmark-5-vector-databases-compared)). This is unverified because the case source was not read. Per-query reporting is emerging, with PQR-Search's fraction-below-threshold and Steiner-hardness ([VLDB PDF](https://vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p4668-wang.pdf)), but it is still grouped by query. An arrival effect lives in **(query, true-neighbour) pairs grouped by the neighbour's insertion time**, and only MN-RU's dataset-as-queries K=1 self-retrieval test comes close to that design.

This makes the benchmark contribution concrete. A fair age-invariance benchmark needs five things. It needs live-set ground truth plus insertion step, and a pairwise miss ratio by age cohort with confidence intervals. It needs query mixes that put new items into true top-k sets in comparable numbers. It needs three insertion orders (random, clustered and real-timestamp) so that age is not confounded with region. And it needs a from-scratch rebuild of the same item set as a control. Real timestamps are the scarce ingredient. The only real-time-ordered ANN workloads found are Quake's Wikipedia trace and DeDrift's YFCC and VideoAds, and CoDEQ's authors complain of "few options for benchmark datasets with an intrinsic notion of streaming order" ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.18335)). An insert-only scientific collection with real arrival snapshots *and* relevance labels, like LongEval-Sci, is rarer than anything in this literature.

## IR has the vocabulary for this bias but never blamed the index

Information retrieval already has the right concepts, retrievability and exposure, but has always pinned the bias on the ranker, the model or the collection. Retrievability (Azzopardi and Vinay, CIKM 2008) measures how a system shapes access to documents regardless of relevance ([DBLP](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/cikm/AzzopardiV08.html)). The closest temporal precedent is Samar et al. (2018). They measure retrievability by document timestamp in a web archive and find that it varies across years, but the bias there comes from the ranker and the version counts ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00799-017-0215-9)). Dense retrievers have been audited for retrievability Gini against BM25 (CIKM 2023) ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3583780.3615221)). T-Retrievability (CIKM 2025) argues that unequal r(d) can reflect topic rather than bias and computes it within topic groups ([arXiv 2508.21704](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21704)). Valentini et al. (EMNLP 2026 Findings) show that bi-encoders learn query-independent document priors that make some relevant documents hard to retrieve ([arXiv 2606.02814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02814)). **No study holds the embedding model fixed and varies only the index (exact vs ANN), and none groups documents by arrival in a growing collection.** That is the IR gap, and the exact-search comparison is what closes it: with the same embeddings for exact and ANN search, model priors such as Valentini's cancel out.

Exposure fairness gives the normative yardstick: equal expected exposure for items of equal relevance (Diaz et al., CIKM 2020) ([arXiv 2004.13157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.13157)). It also gives the sharpest argument. Fair-ranking methods assume the candidate set is fixed. Macdonald and Tonellotto note that in multi-stage dense retrieval the ANN stage chooses the candidates ([arXiv 2108.11480](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.11480v1)), so a document it drops gets zero exposure whatever fairness intervention runs downstream. The theory community has studied "fair" near-neighbour search as uniform sampling among valid neighbours ([ACM TODS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3502867)), which is close in spirit to "every relevant document equally reachable". The term "fairness" itself will draw pushback, since it usually concerns protected groups. "Arrival-time retrievability bias" or "temporal exposure bias" is safer, with producer fairness (new papers, new authors) as an optional hook.

LongEval is the natural venue, but two facts from its own 2026 overview constrain the pitch. First, the collection suits the question: LongEval-Sci 2026 is insert-only by design ("documents are only added ... but never removed"), cumulative across three 2025 snapshots, and nearly every Task 1 run was lexical-first, with only three of 20 runs using a dense component and no participant paper on ANN indexing ([CEUR paper374](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)). Second, the overview finds that "document recency does not appear to be a driving factor of relevance on this testbed" ([CEUR paper374](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4283/paper374.pdf)). A motivation of "new documents matter more" will be quoted back at the authors. The robust motivation is that equally relevant new documents are under-exposed, and that the index is an untested temporal-persistence factor behind LongEval's central observation that effectiveness drifts as collections grow. The paper should also separate index staleness from *model* staleness, the GradNormIR line on when to retrain encoders for evolving corpora ([arXiv 2506.01877](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01877)), because reviewers will conflate them. The aggregate-impact literature helps the "averages hide it" point. Leto et al. find that RAG barely suffers on average from lower ANN recall ([arXiv 2411.07396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07396)), and Lin (ACL 2025 Industry) documents HNSW's build-to-build non-determinism, which our design must handle with multiple seeds ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-industry.61)).

## Production systems mask only the newest window

Industry relevance is real, but it is concentrated in specific architectures. Most production systems are LSM-like and search their freshest data exhaustively. Pinecone has a brute-force memtable for roughly the last 10k writes ([Pinecone](https://www.pinecone.io/learn/slab-architecture/)). Turbopuffer and LanceDB scan unindexed data exhaustively ([turbopuffer](https://turbopuffer.com/docs/architecture); [LanceDB](https://docs.lancedb.com/indexing/reindexing)). Qdrant's appendable segments have no index ([Qdrant](https://qdrant.tech/documentation/ops-optimization/optimizer/)), single-node Chroma keeps a 100-vector buffer ([Chroma](https://docs.trychroma.com/docs/collections/configure)), and Milvus builds an interim index on growing segments ([Milvus](https://milvus.io/docs/configure_querynode.md)). That hides any bias only for minutes to hours of writes. **Single-graph engines insert straight into the main graph**: pgvector HNSW ([pgvector](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)), Vespa's "true real time" mutable HNSW ([Vespa](https://docs.vespa.ai/en/querying/approximate-nn-hnsw)), Cosmos DB's in-place DiskANN ([DevBlogs](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/cosmosdb/azure-cosmos-db-with-diskann-part-4-stable-vector-search-recall-with-streaming-data/)), Redis and Weaviate HNSW. Lucene and Elasticsearch merges copy the largest segment's graph and *insert* the other segments' vectors into it ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/hnsw-graphs-speed-up-merging)), so recently indexed documents become late inserts in an ever-growing graph. That makes Lucene's merge path the highest-leverage production target. Its reverse-link rule (always add, then evict one) is milder than the full re-prune in hnswlib and FAISS ([NeighborArray.java](https://github.com/apache/lucene/blob/main/lucene/core/src/java/org/apache/lucene/util/hnsw/NeighborArray.java); [hnswalg.h](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib/blob/master/hnswlib/hnswalg.h)). That predicts a smaller gradient, which is a cheap and decisive cross-implementation test.

Continuous growth is the norm in production; Pinecone says "very few of our use cases are primarily static corpora" ([Pinecone blog](https://www.pinecone.io/blog/serverless-architecture/)). Yet no vendor across 13 systems publishes recall for newly inserted versus old vectors. Documented complaints are about tombstones (HNSW) and stale centroids (IVF), and AWS documents the IVFFlat rebuild remedy ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/database/running-pgvector-in-production-on-amazon-aurora-postgresql/)). "Recently inserted items are under-retrieved" is not a recognised vendor-level failure mode, which leaves room for a measured contribution and for a monitoring feature no engine offers.

## History-dependence survives review; universal staleness does not

### What is known, what is new, and what is wrong in the original claim

The literature already establishes several things. Global recall depends on insertion order when the order is correlated with the data. Newcomer isolation has been named qualitatively. Churn creates unreachable nodes. IVF centroids and fixed search widths go stale under drift, mostly as cost and aggregate tail loss. Average recall hides per-query failures. The new parts, as far as the searches reach, are these. Ours is the first per-item measurement of retrieval disparity by arrival cohort in a real, insert-only, relevance-labelled growing collection. The base-layer in-degree gradient by insertion decile (28.6 → 13.3) is shown to be a pure position effect, because it is identical under shuffling. Shuffle and reverse controls show that the cohort disparity follows build position, not content (1.28–1.42 as arrived, 1.00–1.03 shuffled, 0.82 reversed). In-degree repair is shown not to remove it fully (1.13–1.26 residual). And we contrast a graph index with a frozen IVF on the same data, where the IVF shows nothing.

The word "staleness" must be dropped or redefined for the graph result. Our shuffle result shows that the graph bias needs no distribution drift: an incremental graph penalises late positions even on exchangeable data. That is entrenchment, not stale fitting. Old nodes' neighbour lists were chosen before the newcomer existed, and the pruning rule protects incumbents. The claim that this is "common to all index families" is refuted by our own IVF arm and by HAKES and Ada-IVF. What survives is a unifying *structure* rather than a unifying *effect*. Every family's retrieval probability is a function of history, but the history variable and the size of the effect differ by family. For incremental graphs it is position, with no drift needed. For partition and quantizer indexes it is drift relative to the training sample. For calibrated search widths it is collection change relative to the calibration set.

| Family | History variable | Expected age effect without drift | Expected under drift | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Incremental graph (HNSW, FreshVamana) | Insertion position | Yes (our data) | Larger? untested | Our unpublished measurements; no published per-age data |
| Batch-refined graph (Vamana two-pass, NSG, CAGRA, DEG) | Build sample only | Predicted none | Predicted none if rebuilt | Untested |
| IVF, frozen centroids | Training sample | None (our data; HAKES) | Cost first, misses at low nprobe (DeDrift) | Partly published, no per-age split |
| PQ/OPQ codecs | Training sample | None | Small (<0.011, DeDrift) | Published |
| Global-range SQ8 | Range sample | None | Clipping of late out-of-range items | Unmeasured |
| Calibrated ef/nprobe, conformal stopping | Calibration set | Graphs degrade even under random insertion (PQR) | Tail grows (PQR, Quake) | Aggregate only |

What would show commonality is a single protocol, the pairwise miss ratio by cohort, crossed over three factors. The first is insertion order (as arrived, shuffled, reversed). The second is drift regime (i.i.d. split, organic LongEval growth, induced topic drift). The third is index family, each against a from-scratch rebuild of the same items. If the ratio is about 1 everywhere except incremental graphs, the paper's claim is "graph history-dependence plus drift-conditional staleness". If induced drift makes frozen IVF and SQ8 show ratios well above 1, a cross-family "history bias" claim becomes defensible, with honest conditions attached.

### The strongest framing for CLEF 2027 and the engine

For the CLEF long paper, the strongest framing is **"The index is a hidden temporal-persistence factor."** As collections grow, dense first-stage retrieval with an incrementally built graph index systematically under-exposes late-arriving documents, while every metric LongEval and the ANN community report stays flat. The contributions would be a metric: the pairwise arrival-cohort miss ratio, reported at neighbour level and at relevance level, alongside retrievability Gini by cohort. Then evidence on LongEval-Sci's three snapshots plus a second real-timestamp collection. Then causal ablations (shuffle, reverse, rebuild). Then the IVF contrast. Then a remedy evaluation. Finally, an evaluation recommendation for LongEval: dense runs should report the index and its build history, and the exact-vs-ANN cohort ratio.

An index-side repair belongs in a separate systems paper or preprint so the two do not double-publish: arrival-invariant linking plus per-age recall telemetry, so that an index stays equally fair to old and new items without rebuilds, with every gain measured.

### Competing work and timing risk

Timing risk is high. GRAB-ANNS's authors could add age stratification in a revision. PQR-Search's authors already run FIFO-age update streams and are one grouping step from a per-age plot. The Lucene #15427 and #15504 threads were active the week before this report. FAISS #5968/#5969 and Chroma #7998 date from November 2026, and the SIGMOD 2026 hubness paper is unread. CLEF 2027 deadlines have not been verified and must be checked. The safest move is an arXiv preprint of the measurement, the metric and the shuffle control within weeks, followed by the full LongEval paper.

### The biggest reviewer objections

The first objection is the weak relevance-level within-query effect. For Epzero the Mantel–Haenszel ratio is 1.28 [0.86, 1.96], and its interval includes 1. FAISS is at 1.70 [1.01, 3.12], barely excluding 1. The pooled 2–3x is therefore partly query composition: the queries whose relevant sets contain new documents are harder for HNSW overall. The answer is to lead with the neighbour-level within-query ratio under shuffle and reverse controls, which needs no qrels and has far more pairs, and to increase relevance-level power.

The second is practical size. The absolute drop rates are 4.0% against 2.0%, and the nDCG gap is negligible. The answer is the exposure argument, plus a dose-response showing how the ratio scales with collection growth, lower ef and more snapshots.

The third is "this is just known order sensitivity". The answer is Elliott and Clark's data-correlated order against our position effect, and FlatNav's access counts against in-degree and recall.

The fourth is label bias. The GPT-OSS-120B labels were pooled from mostly lexical participant runs, and DCTR qrels come from CORE's historical SERPs, so new documents are under-judged. The answer is to report judged@10 by cohort and results under all qrel types.

The remaining objections are one collection with one 2,560-d embedding model; HNSW non-determinism, which needs multiple seeds; hubness and local density as confounds; and "just rebuild". The answer to the last is that a global refine costs about 3.5x the build and still leaves a residual of 1.13–1.26. One internal puzzle needs an explanation before submission: the ratio *rises* from M16 (1.28–1.42) to M32 (1.65), echoing Chroma's M=48 observation. Either the incumbent "shield" gets denser with more slots, or ratios computed over shrinking base rates are unstable. The paper should report absolute differences alongside ratios.

## Conclusion

The research has turned a broad "indexes go stale" thesis into two separate, more precise claims. In incremental graph indexes, arrival-time bias is a property of the construction algorithm. It appears without any drift, follows build position, and is not fully explained by in-degree. Partition and quantizer staleness is drift-conditional, and in the literature it shows up as cost first. The first claim is new and is the paper. The second is mostly known and, on LongEval-Sci, absent, which makes it the paper's control. The most promising scientific lever is the temporal-minority hypothesis. If being outnumbered by differently timed neighbours predicts misses in both directions, the finding generalises from "new documents" to a structural law of incremental graphs that the hubness and churn literatures have not described.

For the engine, the implication is that rebuilds, refine passes and in-degree repair treat the symptom at 3.5x build cost while leaving a residual. An arrival-invariant linking rule, together with per-age recall telemetry, would be both a research contribution and a product differentiator, provided measurements show it removes the residual. The window to stake priority is weeks to months, not a full CLEF cycle, so an early preprint of the measurement and its shuffle control is worth more than a more polished paper later.

## Addendum (23 November 2026): the SIGMOD 2026 hubness paper, read in full

Xu, Dai, Li, Wang, Yue, "Through the Lens of Hubness: A Revisit on Graph-Based Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search [Experiments & Analysis]", Proc. ACM Manag. Data 4(3) (SIGMOD), Article 243, June 2026, doi 10.1145/3802120. Peer-reviewed (received October 2025, accepted February 2026). This was the main unchecked novelty risk; the full text (pp. 1-27) has now been read.

What it establishes:
- In-degree (k-occurrence) skew in proximity graphs is caused by hubness, which it attributes entirely to data geometry: spatial centrality (Lemma 1, after Radovanović et al. 2010) and local intrinsic dimensionality (Sec. 5.2, Table 3). The paper gives theorems for hub capture (Thm 1) and anti-hub isolation (Thm 2).
- Outlier queries, defined as the slowest 50% on a brute-force KNNG, are those whose ground-truth neighbours have low in-degree (Table 6). The paper adds that reachability matters more than raw in-degree: "a target with a higher in-degree remains isolated if its incoming edges connect exclusively to other inaccessible anti-hubs" (Sec. 5.4.2).
- It sorts HNSW, NSG, Vamana, τ-MG, LSG and Vista into a taxonomy of hubness-mitigation strategies (Table 1, Fig. 4).
- It proposes a post-hoc hubness-aware pruning. A factor γ relaxes the MRNG rule (γ > 1) for low-in-degree nodes and tightens it (γ < 1) for high-in-degree nodes (Sec. 5.6). This lowers skewness (Table 7) at an indexing cost of +0.2% for HNSW (Fig. 14). The authors note it is limited by the initial graph: "severely isolated anti-hubs may rarely appear in candidate pools".
- The future work names the construction-side target as open. Sec. 6.2.1 proposes "hubness-native construction" that maintains in-degree statistics during ingestion. Sec. 6.2.3 states that "naive insertions and deletions can introduce new degree imbalances ... Developing bounded-cost update mechanisms that preserve degree regularity and global reachability—without triggering full index rebuilds—remains an important open problem."

What it does not do:
- It never treats insertion order, insertion position or item age as a cause of in-degree. All skew is attributed to geometry.
- Every build is static, with 128 indexing threads, so insertion order is scrambled.
- It has no per-item or per-cohort retrieval analysis and no temporal or growing collection. Its unit is the query (outlier vs typical), not the document.

Consequences for the claim and the plan:
1. **Novelty survives, and gains a sharper edge.** Our shuffled builds show an in-degree gradient by insertion decile (28.6 → 13.3) that is independent of content, so part of HNSW's in-degree variance is positional, not geometric. That bears directly on this paper's framework. Some of the "anti-hubs" in an incrementally built HNSW are late insertions, not peripheral points. A new P0 experiment is to decompose the variance of HNSW in-degree into exact k-occurrence (geometry) and insertion position, and to do the same for the miss probability.
2. **In-degree-aware pruning is now peer-reviewed prior art.** Their γ rule, with a CPU reimplementation from the paper's description, must be a baseline in the remedy bake-off. An engine contribution has to go beyond it: online during ingestion rather than post-hoc, aware of position or arrival, and judged by the cohort miss ratio rather than skewness. Their own caveat, that post-hoc repair cannot reach isolated nodes absent from candidate pools, matches our residual after in-degree repair (1.13-1.26).
3. **"Reachability, not in-degree" is consistent with our residual** and with the hypothesis about who links to new documents, which the running diagnostic tests (in-link source position and similarity, reciprocity, scored versus never-scored misses).
4. **Motivation.** Sec. 6.2.3 can be cited as a peer-reviewed statement that bounded-cost, rebuild-free maintenance of degree regularity and reachability under updates is an open problem.
